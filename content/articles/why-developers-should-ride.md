I write code, build models, and spend an ungodly amount of time staring at documentation in VS Code. But when the screen goes dark, I ride a motorcycle.

It's easy to romanticize riding, especially in India, where the chaos of the road is legendary. But for a software developer, the real value of riding is far more pragmatic. It is the ultimate exercise in real-time, low-latency feedback loops.

### The Attention Economy

As developers, we operate in an attention economy designed to fragment focus. We jump between Slack, a failing CI/CD pipeline, an unread email, and a complicated Git merge conflict.

A motorcycle does not allow multi-threading. You cannot safely parse a complex thought while leaned over on a wet corner doing 80kmph. It demands singular, unyielding focus. It’s one of the few places left where your attention cannot be hijacked by an algorithm.

### Immediate Feedback Loops

In software engineering, the feedback loop between writing bad code and seeing the consequence can take hours or even days. It goes through a compiler, a test suite, a PR review, and eventually breaks production at 3 AM on a Saturday.

On a motorcycle, the feedback loop is milliseconds. If you enter a corner too hot, or fail to read the body language of the truck driver ahead of you, the consequence is immediate and physical. The machine does not forgive "bugs" in your input.

This forced humility translates directly back to engineering. It teaches you to respect the system you are operating within. You stop throwing code at a problem hoping it compiles, and you start methodically planning your inputs. You learn to look further down the road, rather than staring directly at the front wheel.

If you spend your days building complex, abstract systems, get on a motorcycle. It will remind you of the visceral difference between theory and practice.
